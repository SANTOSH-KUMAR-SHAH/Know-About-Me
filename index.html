About the practice

Everyone told me to pick one thing. I picked everything. While my classmates were memorizing formulas for tomorrow's exam, I was reading poetry. Not because I had to. Because the rhythm of words taught me something textbooks never could — how to feel a problem before solving it.

Then I moved to literature. Then to studying hardware — how circuits think, how signals travel, how a machine decides what to do next. Then I started studying people. Their psychology. Their patterns. Why they click where they click. Why they trust what they trust.

And somewhere along the way, I realized something most people never do: The owner of a hospital isn't the best doctor. He's the person who understands how the system works. The hiring. The workflow. The patient experience. The infrastructure that holds everything together while no one's watching. That's what I study. Not just code. Systems.

I was seventeen years old when I looked at my college's digital infrastructure and thought: "I can build this better." So I did. From scratch. Every page, every system, every pixel — designed, developed, and shipped by a kid who wasn't even old enough to vote. The college already had a website. They refused to replace it with mine. That's fine. I preserved it. Not out of spite. Out of patience. Because I know quality doesn't need permission — it just needs time.

How the work begins

I didn't drop out. I outgrew the system. FORMAL EDUCATION: CLASS 12. That's the last line on my formal education. If you're judging already, I get it. Most people stop reading here. But you're not most people, are you?

While my classmates sat in rows absorbing theory they'd forget by next semester, I was building. Not because someone assigned me to. Not because a syllabus told me to. Because the moment I opened a code editor, something clicked that no classroom ever triggered. Obsession.

The kind where you forget to eat because you're chasing a bug at 3am. The kind where everyone around you is studying for an exam, and you're redesigning how an entire system should work. People ask me: "Don't you regret not going the traditional route?" I don't regret it. I just chose a different classroom. One where the exams are deadlines, the textbook is documentation, and the degree is the thing you ship.

The world doesn't care where you learned. It cares what you can do. School taught me one thing: The real education starts when you close the textbook.
